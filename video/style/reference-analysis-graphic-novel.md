# Reference analysis: the nonfiction graphic novel

The house look comes from analysing two nonfiction graphic novels before any frame was drawn: *The Goal: A Business
Graphic Novel* (Zimmerman and Motter, 2017) and *Sapiens: A Graphic History* (Harari, Vandermeulen and Casanave,
2020). This page records the grammar taken from them. It reproduces nothing from either book: no characters, panels,
page designs, drawings or text. Use it as the model for analysing any new reference (`reference-analysis-<name>.md`).

## What to look at in a reference

Ground, line, colour, lettering, composition, how it explains, and how it would move. Then: what we take (grammar)
and what we must not take (content).

## The Goal, as grammar

- **Line:** a confident contour of medium weight; solid blacks do the modelling, with almost no hatching.
- **Colour:** greyscale: paper, two or three flat greys, black. Value and contrast carry the drama, not hue.
- **Lettering:** small boxed captions for place and time; dialogue carries the explanation, with no narrator boxes.
- **Composition:** a large establishing panel with insets; a stacked column of close-ups for a countdown or a
  sequence of approvals; uneven white gutters.
- **How it explains:** a story analogy, and a mentor who asks questions rather than giving answers.

## Sapiens, as grammar

- **Line:** a fine, lively pen line; hatching only as texture; never polished.
- **Colour:** each sequence has its own flat palette of two or three colours, and the palette tells you which mode
  you are in (the present, a myth, a parody).
- **Lettering:** rounded balloons and free-floating captions; parody sequences switch to display type.
- **Composition:** a regular grid broken by splash pages; diagrams drawn into the world (a timeline over a landscape,
  a card game) rather than laid over it.
- **How it explains:** by staging ideas in borrowed formats (a game show, an advert, a trial), with a guide and a
  questioner who asks the audience's questions.

## Moving it without the cheap motion-comic look

- **What fails:** paper-doll puppeting; panels that only slide, drop and fade; art drawn small and shown in close-up;
  art cut out of pages that were composed as whole pages; whiteboard-style line draw-on of everything.
- **What works:** drawing for the camera from the start, in layers; one long page the camera travels; limited,
  motivated movement; line draw-on only for borders, arrows and rules.

## What we take

- **Palette:** greyscale ink on warm paper, plus one accent kept for the idea that matters most (in a film about
  bottlenecks, the current constraint); when that idea moves, the accent moves. A metaphor or parody scene may get
  its own duotone, as Sapiens gives each mode its palette.
- **Line:** clean hairline drawing, solid shapes for weight, texture only where it means something.
- **Lettering:** the voice-over carries the narration and is never subtitled. On screen: short captions (three to six
  words), labels, source tags, an occasional balloon.
- **Composition:** one idea per panel; diagrams drawn as objects in the world (a page, a board, a map), not overlays;
  a column of small figures for a sequence of approvals.
- **Transitions that never cut:** the camera travels one large page; a panel nests inside the last (zoom-through); a
  panel's border grows to become the frame; the accent crosses a gutter and the camera follows it; a door, screen or
  window becomes the next scene; a palette shift during a move.

## What we never take

Characters or their likenesses, names, branding, recognisable page designs or panels, traced or closely paraphrased
art, and the books' text. Style, technique, palette, genre and grammar are free to borrow; a film may name a book and
summarise it with attribution, but draws its own scenes and cast. This is a working rule, not legal advice.
